Act 1 — The decline

Every state is having fewer babies. But some are falling off a cliff.

In 2000, Malaysia recorded over 540,000 live births. By 2023, that number had fallen to around 456,000 — a drop of 16% at the national level. But national averages hide dramatic state-level differences. Perak lost more than a third of its births. Pulau Pinang and Kuala Lumpur lost more than a quarter. Only Terengganu bucked the trend.

Act 2 — The geography

Where you live in Malaysia determines how many children you have.

The geography of fertility mirrors the geography of wealth. Kuala Lumpur — Malaysia's richest territory — now has a birth rate of just 10.7 per 1,000 people, well below the 14.0 needed to sustain population without migration. Meanwhile, Terengganu and Kelantan, among Malaysia's poorest states, maintain birth rates of 21.6 and 19.2 respectively.

Act 3 — The divide

Chinese births have halved. Malay births have grown. This is an economic story.

The national birth decline is not evenly distributed across ethnic groups. Chinese Malaysians, who are disproportionately urban and higher-income, recorded only 44,818 births in 2023 — down from 115,429 in 2000. Their share of total national births collapsed from 21.5% to just 9.8%. This is not a cultural anomaly — it is the same pattern seen in South Korea, Japan, and Taiwan. When incomes rise, families shrink.

"In 2000, 1 in every 5 Malaysian babies was Chinese. By 2023, it was fewer than 1 in 10."

Act 4 — The cause

One number predicts birth rate better than any other: household income.

Plot any Malaysian state's average household income against its birth rate and the relationship is unmistakable — richer states have fewer babies. Every RM1,000 increase in median household income corresponds to roughly a 1.5-point drop in birth rate per 1,000 people. This is not about religion, ethnicity, or culture. It is about the economics of raising children in a modern economy.

Key insight: Selangor (income: RM12,233) has a birth rate of 12.6. Kelantan (income: RM4,885) has a birth rate of 19.2. The income gap is 2.5×. The birth rate gap is 1.5×. Wealth suppresses fertility — everywhere in the world, and Malaysia is no exception.

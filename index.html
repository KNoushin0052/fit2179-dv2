Malaysia · 2000–2023 · Demographic Crisis

Malaysia is running out of babies.

Over 23 years, Malaysia's birth rate has collapsed across every state — but the decline is not equal. Rich states barely reproduce themselves while rural states hold on. Behind the numbers lies an economic story that will reshape the nation.

Act 1 — The Decline

Every state is having fewer babies. But some are falling off a cliff.

In 2000, Malaysia recorded over 540,000 live births. By 2023 that number had fallen to around 456,000 — a drop of 16% nationally. But national averages hide dramatic state-level differences. Perak lost more than a third of its births. Pulau Pinang and Kuala Lumpur lost more than a quarter. Only Terengganu bucked the trend.

"Every single Malaysian state — except one — recorded fewer births in 2023 than in 2000."

Act 2 — The Geography

Where you live in Malaysia determines how many children you have.

The geography of fertility mirrors the geography of wealth. Kuala Lumpur — Malaysia's richest territory — now has a birth rate of just 10.7 per 1,000 people, well below the 14.0 needed to sustain population without migration. Meanwhile, Terengganu and Kelantan, among Malaysia's poorest states, maintain birth rates of 21.6 and 19.2 respectively.

Act 3 — The Divide

Chinese births have halved. Malay births have grown. This is an economic story.

The national birth decline is not evenly distributed across ethnic groups. Chinese Malaysians, who are disproportionately urban and higher-income, recorded only 44,818 births in 2023 — down from 115,429 in 2000. Their share of total national births collapsed from 21.5% to just 9.8%. This is not a cultural anomaly. It is the same pattern seen in South Korea, Japan, and Taiwan. When incomes rise, families shrink.

"In 2000, 1 in every 5 Malaysian babies was Chinese. By 2023, it was fewer than 1 in 10."

Act 4 — The Cause

Follow the money: the states with the most to spare have the fewest babies.

It is tempting to assume families have fewer children because they cannot afford them. The data points the other way. Ranking states by the money left over each month — household income minus spending — the wealthiest territories, Kuala Lumpur and Selangor, have the most to spare, yet the fewest babies. Poorer states such as Kelantan have little left over but far more children. Children are not unaffordable in rich states; they compete with careers, study and lifestyle. The barrier looks like opportunity cost, not poverty — with Terengganu the cultural exception.

Key insight: A Kuala Lumpur household has about RM5,500 left over each month — the most in the country — and the lowest birth rate, 10.7. Kelantan has barely RM1,380 to spare yet a birth rate of 19.2. More money in hand, fewer children: in a wealthy state the real cost of a child is measured in foregone careers and lifestyle, not ringgit.
